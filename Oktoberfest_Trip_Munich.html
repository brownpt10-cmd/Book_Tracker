The big picture

Oktoberfest 2026 runs Saturday 19 September to Sunday 4 October on the Theresienwiese, the 42-hectare meadow locals simply call the Wiesn. It opens at noon on the first Saturday when the Mayor of Munich taps the first keg and shouts "O'zapft is!" — it's tapped! — and pours the ceremonial first Maß.

Some six million visitors come each year to 14 large beer tents and around 20 smaller ones, drinking roughly seven million litres of beer. Entry to the grounds and the tents is free — you pay only for what you eat, drink and ride.

Our window lands squarely in the first, liveliest week. Read on for the plan, the tents, and how to blend in like a proper Münchner.

For Gary & Anna — arriving Thu 9/17

Opening weekend is a different animal

Saturday 9/19 and Sunday 9/20 are the two hardest walk-in days of the entire 16-day festival — this is opening weekend, not a normal weekend.

Sat 9/19 · Opening Day
Tents open 9 AM, but by law no beer pours anywhere until the Lord Mayor taps the keg at noon at Schottenhamel. Be at your tent's door by 8:30 AM, seated by 9, and stay put through the tap — once it's noon the crowd surges hard. Skip Schottenhamel itself; it's hosting the ceremony.
Sun 9/20 · Parade Day
The costume & riflemen's parade runs 10 AM through the city center — worth seeing, but it feeds the crowd straight to the grounds after. Beer pours normally all day, but arrive by 8:30–9 AM regardless; popular tents fill by late morning.

Both days: pick one tent and commit rather than tent-hopping — giving up seats on opening weekend usually means not getting seats again. Beer gardens are the reliable fallback both days: walk-in only, never gated by reservations.

Reservations 101

Free to book but sold in whole tables (8–10 people) with a minimum food-and-drink voucher spend — typically two Maß + half a chicken per person. Popular slots for opening week sell out months ahead.

No reservation?

Totally doable. Arrive early (before 11:00 weekdays), and note tents close their doors when full. Beer garden areas outside the tents don't need bookings and are lovely on a sunny afternoon.

Costs & etiquette
No cover charge for walk-in seating — pay only for food and drink once seated. Minimum consumption (2 beers + a half chicken per person) only applies to reserved tables, not walk-ins.
Bench seating is communal — a group of 2–3 can often slot into open space at a table that isn't fully "free." Ask "Ist hier noch frei?" ("Is this still free?") before sitting.
Stay together at the door — entry gets paced when busy, and going in as a tight group makes it easier to land seats together.
Skip these — near-full reservation tents
Marstall-Festzelt Kufflers Weinzelt Käfer Wiesn-Schänke

Little walk-in room in these three — steer the group elsewhere when picking a target.

Bottom line: no table reservation exists or is realistic for this group this close to the date — tents only book in whole tables of 8–10. Getting there

Travel & flights

✈︎ Into Munich (MUC)

Munich Airport sits northeast of the city. The S1 and S8 S-Bahn lines both reach the Hauptbahnhof / Marienplatz in about 40–45 minutes and run every 10–20 minutes. The Lufthansa Express Bus is a comfy alternative to the centre.

🎫 Getting around

Buy an MVV day pass (Tageskarte) or a group day ticket — cheaper together. For the fest, take U4/U5 to Theresienwiese or the S-Bahn to Hackerbrücke and walk in. Trains get packed at closing time; expect a crush.

🕑 Timing your arrival

Pat lands Monday the 21st — a weekday, so the city is calmer than the opening weekend Gary & Anna just came from. Aim to be checked in by early afternoon so there's time to regroup before the Hofbräuhaus dinner.

💶 Money & SIM

Tents and food stalls are largely cash-only — bring euros and small notes for tips (round up ~10%). An eSIM or EU-roaming plan keeps the group chat alive across the crowds.

🆘 If someone gets separated

EU emergency number is 112 (free, any phone). Oktoberfest runs a "Safe Space" on the grounds for reuniting separated people. Pick a real-world fallback meeting point too — cell service gets spotty in a packed tent. The grounds' official lost & found (Fundbüro) is off-site this year at the Deutsches Museum Verkehrszentrum, Am Bavariapark 5 — items usually surface there a day after being turned in.

Where to eat

Restaurants worth the table

Munich's food scene blends historic beer halls with genuinely great international cooking. Five essential Bavarian spots, five more worth building a meal around, and two beer gardens we'd add to the list.

Essential Bavarian

Augustiner Klosterwirt

4.5 ★
Bavarian · €20–30 · by the Frauenkirche

A classic, cozy two-story spot for sampling local staples — walk-in friendly even without a reservation.

  • Augustiner beer straight from wooden kegs
  • Excellent Schweinshaxe (pork knuckle) and schnitzel
  • Traditional wood-paneled interior

Hofbräuhaus München

Icon
Beer hall · already on Mon's itinerary

The world's most famous beer hall. Touristy, sure, but the history and energy earn it a visit — this is our Monday-night dinner.

  • Grand 16th-century hall with a live brass band
  • Classic Bavarian platters, giant pretzels, Weißwurst
  • Loudest, liveliest room in the "Schwemme" central hall

Wirtshaus in der Au

4.5 ★
German · €20–30 · reservation recommended

A more local feel, with a real specialty: the Bavarian dumpling, done every way imaginable.

  • Big, creative Knödel (dumplings) menu
  • "Jazz-Frühschoppen" jazz brunch on weekends
  • Charming beer garden for warmer afternoons

Ratskeller München

4.4 ★
Bavarian · €20–30 · beneath Marienplatz

A vast, vaulted cellar right under the New Town Hall — grand, a little more formal, still deeply traditional.

  • Sausage sampler, Jaegerschnitzel, pork knuckle all recommended
  • Franconian wines alongside the beer list
  • Several distinct rooms, each with its own historic decor

Gasthaus Isarthor

4.3 ★
Bavarian tavern · €20–30 · near the Isar

A small, no-frills neighborhood tavern — one of the few spots in the center that still feels like one.

  • Simple, high-quality Bavarian home cooking
  • Exclusively Augustiner beer — the locals' favorite brewery
  • Reviews are mixed on food/service — go for the vibe, not a special occasion

Eat & drink

Food & beer primer

The six original breweries

By law, only beer brewed within Munich's city limits to the Reinheitsgebot purity law can be poured at Oktoberfest — and it's been the same six breweries since year one, each served as a strong, golden Festbier by the litre (a Maß).

Augustiner-Bräu

Independent
Founded 1328 · Munich's oldest

The only one still serving from traditional wooden kegs. Its own foundation/family ownership — no corporate parent. Tent: Augustiner-Festhalle.

Hofbräu

Independent
Founded 1589 · the royal court brewery

Started by Duke Wilhelm V, still owned by the Bavarian state government. Tent: Hofbräu-Festzelt (plus the Hofbräuhaus beer hall in town).

Paulaner

Paulaner Gruppe
Founded 1634 · by Paulaner monks

Home of Salvator doppelbock. Tent: Paulaner Festzelt ("Winzerer Fähndl"), topped by a giant rotating beer mug since 1950.

Hacker-Pschorr

Paulaner Gruppe
Traced to 1417 · split, then remerged 1972

Two historic breweries (Hacker and Pschorr) reunited as one. Tent: Hacker-Festzelt, the painted-sky-ceiling "Heaven of Bavaria."

Spaten-Franziskaner

AB InBev
Traced to 1397 · merged with Franziskaner 1922

Also brews Franziskaner Weißbier. Tent: Festhalle Schottenhamel — where the Mayor taps the ceremonial first keg.

Löwenbräu

AB InBev
First recorded 1746 · brewing since the 1300s

Spot the roaring mechanical lion out front. Tent: Löwenbräu-Festzelt.

Six names, really four owners. You asked us to check for duplicates — here's the real one: Paulaner and Hacker-Pschorr are corporate siblings under the Paulaner Brauerei Gruppe (Schörghuber family + Heineken), and Spaten-Franziskaner and Löwenbräu are corporate siblings under AB InBev. Only Augustiner and Hofbräu remain fully independent of both groups. Still six distinct historic recipes and six separate tents, though — nothing to actually remove from the list, just worth knowing who's related to whom.

What to wear

Tracht & packing

👖 Lederhosen

Leather shorts or knee-breeches with a checked shirt and sturdy shoes. They're meant to look worn-in — no need to buy new. Locals really do wear them, so you won't feel out of place.

👗 Dirndl

A fitted bodice, blouse and apron. Which side you tie the apron bow matters: left = single, right = taken, front-centre = virgin or undecided, back = widowed (or a waitress). Tie wisely.

🌦️ Weather, mid-to-late Sept

Highs around 63–66°F (17–19°C), lows around 46–50°F (8–10°C) — warm-ish days, genuinely cold nights. Rain is common (~12 days a month), so pack for scattered showers, not just sun. Tracht works fine under a jacket for the walk in.
